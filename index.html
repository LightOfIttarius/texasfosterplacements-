What the state's investigation data shows

From fiscal year 2016 to 2025, Texas completed 26,077 abuse and neglect investigations in residential child care (general residential operations, residential treatment centers, and child-placing agencies). In 1,416 of them (5.4%), the state found Reason to Believe that abuse or neglect occurred.

Source: Texas DFPS, CCI 4.2 Completed Abuse/Neglect Residential Child Care Investigations, FY2016 to FY2025, from the Texas Open Data Portal. Fiscal years run September to August. Statewide totals only; no home is named.

Each investigation can include several allegations. Across the same years, the state found Reason to Believe for 3,324 allegations in residential child care:

Source: Texas DFPS, CCI 4.3 Types of Abuse by Region, FY2016 to FY2025, residential child care investigations only (general residential operations, residential treatment centers, child-placing agencies, and a small number of other types). These are allegation counts, not children and not investigations, so they cannot be added up to a number of survivors.

What these numbers do not mean

They count investigations, not children. One child can be part of several investigations, and one investigation can involve more than one child, so the number of survivors cannot be worked out from this data.

They include neglect as well as abuse. "Ruled out" does not mean nothing happened, and abuse that was never reported is not counted. Nobody can say how large that missing part is, so this site does not guess.

Because there is no public count of survivors, we do not publish an estimate of the number of victims.

Why this project supports a compensation program

This project's position is that Texas should create a victims' compensation program for people who were abused while in state foster care. The state's own investigation data shows it has found reason to believe abuse or neglect occurred in residential placements in every year from 2016 to 2025.

This is the position of the person who runs this site, not a state finding.

Why Texas needs a victims' compensation program

This project supports a Texas program for people who were abused or neglected while in state foster care. The goal is two things at once: acknowledge and repair the harm, and pay for the treatment and help survivors need to build a stable life. Compensation without care leaves the damage in place. Care without acknowledgment tells survivors it was their private problem.

This page is advocacy. It is the position of the person who runs this site, not a state finding. Facts are linked to their sources.

1. The state has a duty to children it takes into care

In M.D. v. Abbott, a federal judge ruled in 2015 that Texas's management of its foster care system violated the constitutional rights of children in long-term state foster care, because the state exposed them to an unreasonable risk of harm. In 2018 the Fifth Circuit agreed that several constitutional violations had occurred, while narrowing the fixes the lower court had ordered.

When the state takes custody of a child, it takes on responsibility for that child's safety. A program for survivors follows from that responsibility.

Sources: Houston Journal of Health Law & Policy; M.D. v. Abbott, 152 F. Supp. 3d 684 (S.D. Tex. 2015) and 907 F.3d 237 (5th Cir. 2018).

2. The state's own data shows confirmed findings every year

From FY2016 to FY2025, the state found Reason to Believe in 1,416 completed abuse and neglect investigations in residential child care, with findings in every one of those years. See the Survivors tab for the figures and what they do and do not mean.

3. The harm lasts, so treatment matters

Abuse and neglect in childhood can affect health, relationships, work, and education for years. In the Casey Family Programs Northwest Foster Care Alumni Study, which interviewed 479 adults aged 20 to 33 who had been in foster care in Oregon and Washington, 54.4% had a clinical-level mental health problem in the previous 12 months. That study is not about Texas and does not separate abuse in care from other causes, so it shows the scale of need among former foster youth, not what happened to any one person.

Most of what survivors need is ongoing: trauma-informed counseling, medical and dental care, help finishing school or finding work, and stable housing. A one-time payment does not cover that.

Source: ScienceBlog summary of the Casey Family Programs Northwest Foster Care Alumni Study.

4. What exists today, and who it leaves out

The Texas Attorney General runs a Crime Victims' Compensation program for people injured by violent crime in Texas, including child abuse. It is worth trying, and survivors can call 1-800-983-9933 to ask whether their situation qualifies.

The deadline is the problem. Under the program's rules, an application must generally be filed within 3 years of the crime. If the victim was a child, it must be filed within 3 years of when the victim or claimant became aware of the crime, but not after the victim turns 21. The Attorney General can extend the deadline for "good cause," for example if psychological factors prevented filing in time, but that is a judgment call made case by case.

That leaves out many survivors. A person abused in a placement years ago, who is now well past 21, would have to rely on an exception, not a right. Many survivors are not ready to come forward for years, and some only understand what happened to them as adults. A dedicated program for people harmed in state care could remove this cut-off, or set a far longer one, so that help does not end on a date the survivor could not meet.

Source: 1 Tex. Admin. Code § 61.202, Timely Filing an Application. The program's rules can change, and this site is not legal advice. Ask the program or a lawyer about a specific case.

5. A model from another state

In 2022, New Hampshire created a claims fund for people abused as children at its Youth Development Center. Former residents could file claims, and participation was voluntary. Reporting on the fund included a warning from its own administrator that the money set aside might not be enough. The lesson for Texas is to size the fund to the need and keep it open long enough for survivors to come forward.

Sources: NH YDC Claims Administration and Settlement Fund; NHPR.

What a Texas program could include

A draft for discussion, written by the person who runs this site. Survivors should shape the real design.

  • Treatment first: paid trauma-informed therapy and medical care, with no short time limit.
  • Direct compensation for harm, separate from treatment.
  • No fixed cut-off, or a far longer one, so survivors who are now well past 21 are not shut out. Many are not ready to come forward for years.
  • Low-barrier, confidential applications that do not require survivors to relive their story repeatedly.
  • Practical help: education, job training, and housing support.
  • Survivor voices in the design, and an administrator independent of the agencies being reviewed.
  • Public reporting on how many people are helped and how much is spent.

The safety score

The safety score runs from 0 to 100. It compares each open general residential operation in Texas with the others, using state inspection findings. Each finding is weighted by how serious the state rated it (5 points for the most serious down to 1 point for the least), the weighted total is divided by the number of inspections, and homes are ranked. A higher score means fewer weighted findings per inspection than other Texas homes. The score is relative: it shows how a home compares with others, not whether it is safe in an absolute sense.

The inspection record rating

Homes with a score also get a plain rating of Good, Average, or Poor based on fixed standards. A home with a state corrective or adverse action on record can never be rated Good. The rating is based on state inspection findings only. It does not describe quality of care, staffing, or daily life for children, because that information is not published.

Who gets a rating

Only homes with 5 or more inspections get a score and a rating. Homes with fewer show a preliminary Early signs note instead. It is based on very little information, new homes often receive several findings at their first inspections, and it can change quickly. It is not a score and should not be treated as one.

External findings

Some homes list findings from public sources such as court monitor reports, state actions, and news reporting. Each one shows its category, a summary, its source, and its date. These are separate from the score and rating. They are matched to homes by name or operation number, so a match could be wrong, and a finding may be out of date. Please check the linked source, and tell us about any error so it can be corrected.

What is not in the score

Staff-to-child ratio, staff turnover, discipline and restraint practices, dietary information, education program quality, therapy actually provided, extracurricular activities, community involvement, and building and environment safety are all Not reported. The state does not publish them, so we do not guess. The "Licensed services" and "Licensed to treat" tags show what a home is approved to offer, not how well it does it.

Limits to keep in mind

  • The state's inspection counts cover an unknown time period, so homes that have been open longer may have more findings on record.
  • The state's online compliance history leaves out violations until a home has had due process, so recent problems may be missing.
  • The state says its violation weights are not intended to be used as a ranking or score. This site is an independent analysis and is not endorsed by the State of Texas.
  • Scores and ratings refresh weekly from the state's open data and may shift slightly.
  • No information about individual children is collected or shown.
